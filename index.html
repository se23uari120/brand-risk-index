              How the brand risk score works

              Everything on this page is generated from the same data and code that produced today's scores, so the numbers in the examples are real.

              In one minute

              When something damages a company's reputation — a product recall, a regulator's probe, a fatal accident, a guidance cut — it shows up in two places: investors sell the shares more than the market explains, and the press writes negative stories. This monitor measures both every trading day for every tracked company and condenses them into one number from 0 (nothing unusual) to 100 (severe, company-specific stress).

              1 · CollectDaily NSE share prices (Yahoo Finance) and recent headlines (Google News + Indian business outlets).
              2 · MeasureFive signals, each turned into 0–100 risk points: three from the stock, two from news.
              3 · WeighAHP turns expert judgements ("market reaction matters most") into weights.
              4 · CombineTOPSIS measures how close the company sits to the worst case vs. the safest case.
              5 · TrackThe score is stored every day, so you can watch it rise and fall.

              The key idea: only company-specific stress counts. If the whole market or the whole sector falls, that is not a problem with one brand — so those moves are removed before anything is scored.

              Which companies

              Each company is matched with its sector peers (other tracked companies in the same sector). Their average daily move is used to strip out sector-wide swings. The sector groups follow NSE's industry classification, with banks kept apart from other financial companies and power apart from oil and gas. Sectors with fewer than three tracked companies (Telecom, Transport & Logistics) are compared with the NIFTY 50 index only. A company listed only recently (for example Vedanta Aluminium, demerged in June 2026) is scored once it has 60 trading days of prices, and its panel notes the shorter history.

              To track another NSE company, add its NSE symbol, a news search query and the name variants used in headlines to backend/data/watchlist.json.

              The five signals

              Every signal answers one plain question and is converted to risk points from 0 to 100. Stock signals are always available; news signals are 0 when there are no relevant headlines, so the score then rests on stock behaviour alone.

              Weighting the signals — AHP

              The Analytic Hierarchy Process (Saaty, 1980) avoids picking weights out of thin air. Instead we answer simple pairwise questions — "How much more important is market reaction than news tone?" — on a 1–9 scale (1 = equally important, 2 = slightly more, 3 = moderately more, 5 = strongly more). The answers fill the matrix below; each cell says how much more important the row is than the column, and the mirror cell holds the reciprocal.

              From judgements to weights

              The weights are the matrix's principal eigenvector (scaled to add up to 100%). Intuitively: a criterion that wins its comparisons consistently ends up with a large share. AHP also checks the judgements don't contradict each other (e.g. A > B and B > C but C > A) using the consistency ratio; below 0.10 is acceptable.

              Combining them — TOPSIS

              TOPSIS (Hwang & Yoon, 1981) scores an option by its distance to an ideal and an anti-ideal. Here the ideal (A⁺) is the safest possible company — 0 on every signal — and the anti-ideal (A⁻) is the worst possible — 100 on every signal.

              1. Scale each signal to 0–1: r = points ÷ 100
              2. Apply the AHP weight: v = w × r
              3. Distance to the safe ideal: D⁺ = √Σ v²
              4. Distance to the worst case: D⁻ = √Σ (w − v)²
              5. Risk score: 100 × D⁺ ÷ (D⁺ + D⁻) — 0 at the safe ideal, 100 at the worst case.

              Why TOPSIS and not a simple average?

                Risk levels

                The score is grouped into three levels. The cut-offs are deliberately strict: most large companies on most days should be Low, so a Moderate or High flag means something genuinely unusual is happening.

                Issue types and the 90% range

                What kind of issue is it?

                Every headline is also given an issue type by a transparent rule-based classifier — Theft & Crime, Regulatory Action, Fraud & Governance, Tax Dispute, Legal & Litigation, Data & Cyber, Safety & Product, Service Outage, Labour & Protest, Leadership Change, Financial Distress, ESG & Environment, Market & Earnings — and the regulators it names (RBI, SEBI, USFDA, …) are recorded. A company's main issue is the type that carries most of its recency-weighted negative coverage. The issue type explains the score; it does not change it.

                How sure is the score?

                The news part depends on which stories happened to be picked up, so each company's score is recomputed for 200 random resamples of its week's headlines (drawn with replacement). The middle 90% of those scores is shown as the 90% range. A narrow range means the result does not hinge on one or two stories.

                Robustness and validation

                • MCDM tab: today's ranking is recomputed with objective CRITIC weights, a 50/50 blend and equal weights; every AHP judgement is moved one step on the 1–9 scale; each weight is changed by ±10–50%; and 1,000 random weightings are drawn around the AHP weights. The rank agreement (Spearman) and the number of companies that change level show how much the results depend on the weights.
                • Validation tab: a pilot back-test scores 12 documented Indian incidents (RBI action on Paytm and Kotak, the Hindenburg report on Adani, the JLR cyber attack, …) point-in-time in the incident week and 60 days earlier, and compares the index with stock-only, news-only, average-sentiment and headline-count baselines.
                • Sentiment check: with the server running, headlines can be hand-labelled (the model's answer hidden) to measure the accuracy of the word list and FinBERT.
                • Unit tests: py -3 -m unittest discover -s backend/tests checks the AHP weights and consistency, TOPSIS, levels, every signal formula, the stock model on synthetic data (a sector-wide fall must not be flagged), news relevance, sentiment, the classifier, CRITIC and the sensitivity analysis.

                Daily tracking

                • When: an automated job (GitHub Actions) runs every weekday at 16:45 IST, after the NSE closes at 15:30, then commits the new data so this site updates by itself. With the Python server running, the scores are also refreshed every hour.
                • Point-in-time: each day is scored using only prices up to that day's close and headlines published up to that day — exactly what the model would have said at the time.
                • Backfill: on the very first run the last 30 trading days are scored so the trend lines are not empty.
                • History: past days are frozen as originally scored; only the latest five days are refreshed (late news, corrected prices). Up to 400 trading days are kept.

                Static site vs live server

                The same dashboard runs in two ways:

                • Static (GitHub Pages, or opening index.html): shows the stored results in js/data.js, updated by the daily job. Everything except Company Search, the watchlist editor and the server parts of the Validation tab works.
                • Live server (start.bat / ./start.sh / Docker): re-scores every hour, adds Company Search for any NSE-listed company (now or as of a past day), adding companies to the watchlist, re-running the back-test, labelling headlines and scoring any headline. A green Live badge appears in the header; click it to refresh now.
                • Static site + free hosted server (GitHub Pages + Render, see render.yaml): the stored daily results load instantly. When they are more than 15 minutes old, opening the page makes the free, read-only server download fresh prices and headlines and re-score every company; an Updating… badge shows, and the page swaps in the new scores by itself a few minutes later. The server also runs Search and the headline scorer. It sleeps when nobody visits, so waking it takes about a minute. It cannot load FinBERT, so headlines the daily FinBERT run already scored keep their FinBERT score and newer ones are scored with the finance word list until the next daily run.

                Data sources & limitations

                • Prices: daily split- and dividend-adjusted closes and volumes from Yahoo Finance's public chart data for NSE listings (SYMBOL.NS) and the NIFTY 50 index (^NSEI). It is an unofficial source and occasionally late or revised.
                • News: Google News RSS search per company (which also carries Moneycontrol, Economic Times, Mint and others), plus the RSS feeds of Business Standard, Economic Times, Livemint, Hindu BusinessLine, CNBC-TV18, NDTV Profit and Indian Express for the latest three days. A headline must name the company (and not a similarly named one); market wrap-ups ("Sensex, Nifty fall…") and duplicates are removed. Only headlines are analysed, not full articles. Moneycontrol's own news sitemap is used too; some networks are refused (HTTP 403), and then Moneycontrol stories still arrive through Google News.
                • A stock fall is not always a brand problem. Results, guidance or analyst downgrades also move prices. Read the headlines in each company's panel to judge the cause.
                • A Low score does not mean "no reputational issue". If the market shrugs off bad news, the stock signals stay quiet; the "Most negative news" list on the Overview catches those cases.
                • Not investment advice. This is an analytical monitoring tool for brand and reputation risk.

                Changes compared with the report

                The first version of this project (the report's Tables III–V) scored companies from news alone with four criteria. This version keeps the same AHP + TOPSIS framework and everything added for the report's remaining work, and changes the evidence:

                • Criteria: Severity / Reach / Recoverability / Criticality (news-derived; C3 and C4 were formulas of C1 and C2) are replaced by five independently measured signals — three from company-specific stock behaviour and two from news. This also addresses the report's open item "independent indicators for C3/C4".
                • Weights: a new 5×5 AHP matrix (CR 0.003) instead of the 4×4 matrix (CR 0.0115).
                • Levels: Low < 30, Moderate 30–50, High ≥ 50 instead of Low < 40, Moderate 40–70, Critical ≥ 70, because the stricter, company-specific evidence produces lower everyday scores; the prescribed actions are the same.
                • Kept from the report's work: time decay of headlines, neutral headlines not diluting the news tone, the two-headline damping, fixed TOPSIS reference profiles, the crisis-event classifier, the bootstrap range, CRITIC weights, the sensitivity analysis, the incident back-test, the FinBERT labelling check, Company Search and the setup / Docker deployment.

                Run it yourself

                Full app (live server, search, FinBERT): run setup.bat once, then start.bat, and open http://localhost:8000. On macOS / Linux use ./setup.sh and ./start.sh; with Docker, docker build -t brand-risk . then docker run -p 7860:7860 brand-risk.

                Just the pipeline needs Python 3.10+ and three packages (numpy, pandas, requests):

                pip install -r backend/requirements.txt
                py -3 backend/run_pipeline.py

                Then open index.html. Other commands: --backtest re-runs the incident back-test, --search Swiggy scores any NSE company, --eval-ahp prints the AHP matrix and weights, --test-headline "…" scores one headline.
